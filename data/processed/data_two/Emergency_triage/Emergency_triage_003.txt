A seizure is a sudden episode of abnormal electrical activity in the brain that can cause shaking, stiffness, staring, confusion, or loss of consciousness. A person may have jerking movements of the arms or legs, may appear blank or unresponsive, or may be briefly confused after the episode. Seizures can be caused by fever, head injury, epilepsy, infection, or other medical conditions.

During a seizure, the immediate priority is to keep the person safe. The person should be moved away from danger such as water, sharp objects, or hot surfaces. Place them on their side if possible if they are not awake, loosen tight clothing around the neck, and avoid forcing anything into the mouth. Do not restrain the person or hold them down. The seizure itself usually lasts a short time, but the person may need time to recover and may be confused or sleepy afterwards.

After the seizure, the person may be drowsy, confused, or unable to speak clearly. A medical assessment is needed if the seizure is the first one, lasts longer than expected, occurs repeatedly, or is associated with trouble breathing, injury, high fever, or reduced consciousness. A seizure with a persistent breathing problem or no return to normal alertness is a medical emergency.

Unresponsiveness is another emergency sign. A person who cannot be roused, is not responding normally, or is confused after a seizure needs urgent assessment. This can indicate a serious underlying problem such as a severe infection, head injury, low blood sugar, or another life-threatening cause.

Children and infants can have fever-related seizures, which can be frightening but may also be a sign of a serious underlying illness. In infants, seizures or unresponsiveness may signal an urgent problem and should prompt immediate evaluation. A person with a seizure who has severe breathing problems, blue lips, or prolonged confusion should go to the nearest emergency service immediately.

Caregivers and bystanders should be calm and focused on safety and rapid help. Calling for emergency assistance is important when there is not a clear recovery, a severe injury, or a seizure in a very young child. After the event, the person should be reviewed by a healthcare professional, especially if the episode is unusual or recurrent.
