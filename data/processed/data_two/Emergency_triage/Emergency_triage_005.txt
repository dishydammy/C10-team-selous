A severe allergic reaction, or anaphylaxis, is a rapidly progressing emergency. It can occur after a food, insect sting, medicine, latex exposure, or another trigger. Symptoms may include swelling of the lips or tongue, throat tightness, wheezing, trouble breathing, widespread rash, dizziness, fainting, confusion, nausea, vomiting, or stomach cramps. Anaphylaxis can become life-threatening within minutes.

A person who has sudden breathing difficulty or swelling of the face, lips, or throat needs immediate emergency care. They may be unable to speak in full sentences or may feel that their throat is closing. In children, sudden wheezing, persistent vomiting, or a severe widespread rash after exposure to a food or sting should also be treated as an emergency.

People who know they are at risk of severe allergic reactions may have prescribed emergency medication, such as an epinephrine auto-injector. In an emergency, it should be used according to the person’s plan and trained instructions. The person should still seek urgent medical care after use because symptoms can recur or worsen. If the person is not responding, breathing is difficult, or they are losing consciousness, emergency services should be called immediately.

Prevention includes identifying triggers, avoiding known allergens, and teaching caregivers and school staff how to respond. In school, teachers and staff should know which children have severe allergies and how to access medication quickly. Families should also ensure emergency plans are up to date and that medication is not expired or missing.

Severe allergic reactions can also happen without a known prior history. Any sudden swelling, widespread hives, vomiting, breathing difficulty, or collapse after an exposure should be treated as an emergency. Delays in care are dangerous.
