Breathing difficulty is a medical emergency when it is severe or associated with blue lips, severe distress, or fainting. It can arise from asthma attacks, pneumonia, allergic reactions, choking, severe infections, trauma, or other medical conditions. The person may be breathing fast, struggling to inhale or exhale, making extra effort, or unable to speak normally.

Common signs of breathing difficulty include fast breathing, wheezing, noisy breathing, chest tightness, coughing, drooling, or a feeling of not getting enough air. In children and infants, signs may include grunting, pulling in the ribs or neck muscles, nostrils flaring, or unusual tiredness. A person who is speaking only in short phrases, unable to lie flat, or gasping for air needs urgent attention.

Some situations are life-threatening and require immediate emergency care. These include severe shortness of breath, blue or grey lips or tongue, collapse, confusion, severe wheezing, chest pain, or trouble swallowing. Choking with a blocked airway is also an emergency. People who are in severe distress should not wait for reassurance or try to manage the problem alone.

People with asthma, pneumonia, a chest infection, or an allergic reaction may have breathing problems that worsen quickly. For asthma, early treatment and a written action plan are important, but a severe attack with severe distress or difficulty speaking is an emergency. For allergic reactions, swelling of the throat or lips can rapidly compromise the airway.

If a person has difficulty breathing at rest, after a sting or insect bite, after smoke exposure, or after choking, they should seek emergency care immediately. If there is a known inhaler, the person may use it only if it was prescribed and the instructions are known, but severe symptoms still require urgent assessment. Caregivers should remain with the person and keep them as calm as possible while arranging urgent help.

Breathing difficulty should never be dismissed as anxiety or mild wheeze when the person is visibly struggling. Early recognition of danger signs saves lives. In schools and community settings, staff should know how to recognise severe respiratory distress and activate urgent help quickly.
