Dehydration happens when a person loses more fluid than they replace. It can be caused by diarrhoea, vomiting, fever, poor intake, heat exposure, or heavy sweating. Severe dehydration is a serious condition because the body may not have enough fluid to maintain circulation, kidney function, and mental alertness. It can affect infants, children, pregnant people, older adults, and anyone with intense vomiting or diarrhoea.

Early signs of dehydration include thirst, dry mouth, dizziness, reduced urination, dark urine, tiredness, and dry skin. More serious signs include fast heart rate, weakness, confusion, very little or no urine, sunken eyes, cold hands and feet, severe weakness, rapid breathing, and lethargy. In children and infants, a sunken fontanelle, poor feeding, few wet diapers, dry lips, and unusual sleepiness are concerning.

People with severe dehydration may be unable to drink enough fluids, may feel faint, or may be not making urine. This is a medical emergency and requires urgent care. People with severe weakness, confusion, fainting, or collapse should receive urgent assessment. Severe dehydration can progress to shock, which is life-threatening.

Prevention is important. Families and communities should prioritize safe drinking water, oral rehydration solutions for diarrhoea, and careful monitoring of people who are vomiting or have fever. In hot weather or during illness, fluids should be offered frequently. Recognising danger signs early allows quicker treatment and can prevent death, especially in infants and children.

Healthcare teams may recommend oral rehydration therapy when a person can drink and is not severely unwell. In more severe cases, fluid replacement may need to be done in a hospital or clinic setting. People should not wait for symptoms to become extreme before seeking help.
