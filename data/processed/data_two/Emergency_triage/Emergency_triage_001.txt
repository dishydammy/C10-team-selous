Fever in infants and young children is common but can be a sign of serious illness. A fever is usually recognized as a temperature above the usual range, and it may be caused by a viral infection, bacterial infection, or another medical condition. The level of concern depends on the child’s age, behaviour, and other symptoms, not just the temperature number alone.

For infants under three months, even a mild fever can be significant and should be assessed promptly because they can become ill quickly and may not show clear symptoms. In older infants and young children, fever is often caused by common infections, but fever with lethargy, poor feeding, breathing difficulty, persistent vomiting, rash, or dehydration needs urgent review.

Parents and caregivers should look at the child’s activity, feeding, breathing, hydration, responsiveness, and skin colour. Child-friendly danger signs include difficulty waking, unusual sleepiness, poor feeding, a weak cry, cool or mottled skin, severe lethargy, breathing fast or hard, and a rash that does not fade when pressed. If the child is floppy, difficult to rouse, or has a seizure, emergency care is needed immediately.

A fever is not always dangerous, but a child with persistent high fever or fever accompanied by persistent vomiting, abdominal pain, stiff neck, rash, or reduced urine output should be assessed sooner. Dehydration can occur if a child is not drinking well, has diarrhoea, or is vomiting repeatedly. In this age group, dehydration can develop quickly.

At home, caregivers can monitor fluids, breathing, alertness, and signs of worsening illness. If the child cannot keep fluids down, has fewer wet diapers or nappies, or is excessively sleepy, they should seek medical support. Parents should avoid giving medicines without checking instructions, especially for infants, and should never guess doses.

The key principle is that older children with mild fever may be managed with rest and fluids, but younger infants and any child with danger signs need professional assessment. The danger is not the fever itself but the underlying illness and the child’s ability to stay hydrated and stable.
