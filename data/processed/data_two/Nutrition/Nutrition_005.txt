Malnutrition refers to a poor nutritional state caused by inadequate intake, poor absorption, or increased nutrient needs. It can include undernutrition, deficiency diseases, and sometimes excess weight or obesity. In public health, it most often refers to inadequate energy, protein, or micronutrient intake that affects growth, immunity, or body function. Nutritional deficiencies can arise from low food quality, poor diet variety, chronic illness, poverty, food insecurity, or difficulty absorbing nutrients.

Common problems include iron deficiency, vitamin A deficiency, iodine deficiency, zinc deficiency, and protein-energy undernutrition. Iron deficiency can lead to fatigue, weakness, reduced concentration, and anaemia. Vitamin A deficiency can affect vision and immunity. Iodine deficiency can affect thyroid function and development. Protein-energy malnutrition can lead to poor growth, muscle wasting, weakness, and poor immunity. These problems may be more serious in children, pregnant people, and people with chronic illness.

The causes are often complex and may involve poor access to food, repeated infections, low dietary diversity, chronic disease, or social factors. For example, frequent diarrhoeal illness can worsen nutritional status because it can reduce intake and increase nutrient losses. In communities facing food insecurity, children and pregnant people are especially vulnerable. Identifying malnutrition early is important because it can affect growth, immunity, and long-term health.

Signs of malnutrition can vary. In adults, they may include unintentional weight loss, muscle weakness, poor appetite, fatigue, brittle hair, dry skin, or swollen feet. In children, signs may include poor growth, thin arms and legs, swelling, developmental delay, or frequent infections. A person with severe weakness, confusion, severe dehydration, or difficulty breathing needs urgent medical review. Severe malnutrition can cause life-threatening complications and requires careful clinical management.

Prevention focuses on improved diet quality, breastfeeding support, safe complementary feeding, food security, and access to health services. Fortified foods, micronutrient supplementation, and public-health programmes can help where there is a risk of deficiency. Community programmes should also be designed to support vulnerable groups, especially infants, children, pregnant people, and people with chronic disease.

People with poor appetite, repeated vomiting, swallowing difficulty, chronic diarrhoea, or digestive disease may need medical assessment because their dietary needs may not be met even if they eat regular meals. Support should not be limited to food advice alone when a person may have an underlying medical reason for poor nutrition.

The key public-health message is that malnutrition is not just “not eating enough”; it is a broader problem of nutrient adequacy, health, and access. Early identification and support can prevent serious harm and improve long-term health outcomes.
