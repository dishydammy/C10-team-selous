Child nutrition is essential for growth, brain development, immunity, and school performance. Children need regular, varied meals and appropriate portion sizes relative to age and activity level. Poor nutrition can affect attention, learning, mood, and physical growth, while growth faltering or undernutrition may signal an underlying medical or social problem that needs attention.

A child’s diet should include energy, protein, fibre, calcium, iron, zinc, and vitamins. These nutrients come from foods such as beans, lentils, eggs, dairy or fortified alternatives, vegetables, fruits, and whole grains. Regular meals with fruit and vegetables support good health. Sweetened drinks, very salty snacks, and highly processed foods should not become the main source of calories.

Children are more vulnerable to micronutrient deficiencies than adults because they are growing rapidly. Iron deficiency can lead to fatigue, poor concentration, and reduced immunity. Vitamin A deficiency can affect vision and immunity. Iodine deficiency can affect the brain and growth. Zinc deficiency can contribute to poor healing and infections. These problems can be prevented or reduced through diet diversity, fortification, and community programmes when appropriate.

School meals and home routines can strongly shape child nutrition. In many settings, children eat large portions of food at school or after school, so ensuring access to nutrient-rich foods matters. Schools can also support healthy habits by offering safe, balanced meals and discouraging frequent sugary drinks or fast-food options. Families benefit from practical education on affordable, balanced meals and portion sizes.

Caregivers should look for signs of inadequate nutrition, such as persistent tiredness, slow growth, poor appetite, repeated infections, irritability, or poor attention in class. A child who is losing weight, has a swollen abdomen, recurrent diarrhoea, or severe weakness needs medical attention. Growth monitoring can help identify problems early in childhood.

A child’s dietary needs vary by age and stage. Young children need nutrient-dense food and regular feeding, while older children may need more energy for activity. Food should be safe, age-appropriate, and culturally acceptable. Parents and caregivers should not rely on sugary drinks or “supplements” as substitutes for meals. If there are concerns about a child’s diet, a community health worker or primary care professional can help assess risks.

The best nutrition plan is one that is consistent, realistic, and includes the child’s usual food culture. It should also support safe feeding, good hydration, and access to healthcare when there are signs of undernutrition or growth issues.
