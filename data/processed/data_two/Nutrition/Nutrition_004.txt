Nutrition during pregnancy is important for both the mother and the baby. The body needs enough energy, protein, folate, iron, calcium, iodine, and other nutrients to support fetal growth, placental function, blood volume expansion, and maternal health. A balanced diet can reduce the risk of complications such as anaemia, low birth weight, and poor maternal recovery after birth.

A pregnant person should aim to eat regular meals with a variety of foods. Protein sources such as beans, lentils, eggs, fish when appropriate, lean meat, and dairy or fortified alternatives help support tissue growth. Fruit and vegetables provide fibre and vitamins. Whole grains provide energy and minerals. Iron-rich foods and calcium-rich foods are particularly important, and foods that help iron absorption, such as fruits rich in vitamin C, may be useful when combined appropriately with meals.

Folate is important before and during early pregnancy because it supports normal fetal development. Pregnant people may need folate supplementation depending on their situation and professional advice. Iron is also commonly discussed because pregnancy increases iron requirements and deficiency can contribute to tiredness, weakness, and poor health. Iodine supports brain and thyroid development. A clinician or dietitian can guide intake based on the person’s needs, diet, and medical history.

Nutrition is not only about adding extra calories; quality matters. Some people need more nutrients but not necessarily a large increase in total calories. A diet that is highly restricted or lacking in variety may need review, particularly if there is nausea, vomiting, food aversions, access problems, or chronic illness. More frequent smaller meals can help when nausea makes eating large meals difficult.

Pregnant people should also pay attention to safe food handling. This includes washing fruit and vegetables, cooking meat thoroughly, avoiding unpasteurized foods, and practising safe food storage. Food safety is especially important because infections during pregnancy can be more serious. Pregnant people should also talk to a clinician before using herbal products, unregulated supplements, or medicines because some may not be safe.

Warning signs of poor nutrition or pregnancy-related risk include severe weakness, dizziness, breathlessness, persistent vomiting, weight loss, swelling of the legs or face, or signs of dehydration. If a pregnant person has significant symptoms or concerns about their diet, they should seek healthcare support. Health professionals may also assess for anaemia, blood pressure issues, and gestational diabetes if relevant.

The most effective nutrition advice is individualized and realistic. Good prenatal nutrition supports the health of the mother and the baby and can improve recovery after delivery. Family and community support can help pregnant people access nutritious food, prepare balanced meals, and attend antenatal visits.
