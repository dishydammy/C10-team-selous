Infant feeding is a critical part of early growth, immunity, and development. The best feeding method depends on the infant’s age, health, and the mother’s circumstances. In many settings, exclusive breastfeeding for the first months of life is recommended when it is feasible and the mother and baby are well-supported. Breast milk provides a balance of nutrients and antibodies that help protect the infant from certain infections.

Breastfeeding benefits the baby and can also support the mother’s recovery after birth, but it requires adequate support. Good breastfeeding practices include early initiation when safe and possible, feeding on demand or according to the baby’s cues, ensuring a good latch, and responding to hunger signs such as rooting, sucking motions, and crying. Frequent feeding helps maintain milk supply and ensures the baby receives enough milk.

A baby who is not feeding well may show warning signs such as poor latch, weak sucking, fewer wet nappies, unusual sleepiness, poor weight gain, persistent jaundice, fever, or dehydration. A sudden inability to feed, vomiting, or breathing difficulty should prompt an assessment. In many cases, lactation support from community health workers, nurses, or infant-feeding professionals helps resolve common problems.

For infants who cannot be breastfed or when breastfeeding is not possible, formula feeding may be required. Formula must be prepared safely and according to the instructions. Over-diluting or over-concentrating formula can harm the infant. It is also important to sterilize feeding equipment when guidance requires it, and to avoid unsafe feeding practices such as giving honey to infants under one year because it can cause serious illness.

Complementary feeding begins when the infant is developmentally ready, usually around months when breast milk alone is no longer sufficient. The introduction of age-appropriate foods should be gradual and should include iron-rich foods, soft textures, and a range of nutrient-dense foods. Avoiding added sugar and salt is important. Foods should be introduced one at a time to help identify reactions, but this should be done in a way that is practical and culturally appropriate.

The infant should be watched for signs of intolerance, such as rash, vomiting, diarrhoea, or hives after a new food. A clinician should be consulted if feeding problems persist or if the baby has poor growth, repeated vomiting, persistent diarrhoea, or swollen abdomen. Feeding plans should consider the infant’s weight gain, health, and the family’s situation.

The mother’s nutrition and wellbeing also matter. She needs enough fluids, energy, and nutrients to support milk production, recovery, and long-term health. If a mother is unwell, depressed, or unable to breastfeed, support should be provided without guilt or blame. Infant feeding decisions should be individualized and based on what is safe, feasible, and medically appropriate.
