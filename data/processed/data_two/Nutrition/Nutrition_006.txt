Food safety, safe water, and hydration are central to preventing infection and maintaining health. Unsafe food or water can cause diarrhoeal illness, food poisoning, dehydration, and serious complications, especially in young children, pregnant people, and people with chronic illness. Hydration supports digestion, circulation, body temperature control, and physical and cognitive function.

Food safety starts with clean hands, safe storage, and proper cooking. Food should be prepared with clean utensils, surfaces, and containers. Raw meat, poultry, eggs, and seafood should be kept separate from ready-to-eat foods. Food should be stored at safe temperatures, and leftovers should be reheated appropriately. People should avoid eating foods that look spoiled, smell off, or have been left out too long in warm conditions.

Safe water is essential for drinking, cooking, cleaning, and washing food. If water may be contaminated, it may need to be treated, filtered, boiled, or otherwise made safe according to local public-health advice. Water storage containers should be cleaned regularly and covered to prevent contamination. Children and infants are especially at risk from contaminated water because dehydration from diarrhoeal illness can escalate quickly.

Hydration is not only about drinking water. It also includes replacing fluid losses from sweating, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, or exercise. Mild dehydration can cause thirst, dry mouth, reduced urine, headache, and tiredness. More severe dehydration causes confusion, dizziness, fainting, rapid heartbeat, and reduced urination. In severe cases, emergency care is needed.

People often ask whether sports drinks or oral rehydration solutions are needed. These can be helpful in certain situations, especially when there has been vomiting, diarrhoea, or heavy sweating. However, plain water is not always enough for replacement of salts, particularly with diarrhoea or dehydration. Oral rehydration solutions are designed for this purpose, and they are often more appropriate than sugary drinks alone. The exact best choice depends on the situation and local guidance.

Food and water safety during pregnancy deserves special attention. Pregnant people should avoid unpasteurized dairy products, undercooked meat, and foods with a risk of contamination. Safe hydration supports circulation and reduces fatigue. In infants, bottle feeding and formula preparation must follow hygiene instructions to reduce infection risk. Small children should always be supervised around water and choking hazards.

If a person is vomiting repeatedly, has severe diarrhoea, cannot drink, or has signs of dehydration, they should seek medical help. This is especially important for infants, children, pregnant people, and people with chronic disease. The risks from infection and dehydration can become serious quickly.
