Medication side effects are unwanted effects that can happen when a medicine works as intended but also causes an additional reaction in the body. Some side effects are mild and temporary, while others are serious enough to require medical review. Adverse reactions are broader than side effects and include harmful or unexpected reactions to a medicine, including allergic reactions or severe toxicity.

Mild side effects may include nausea, dizziness, drowsiness, dry mouth, diarrhoea, headache, or skin irritation. These may be manageable and may settle as the body adapts, but they should still be reported to a clinician or pharmacist if they are severe, persistent, or disruptive. People should not stop taking a prescribed medicine suddenly unless a healthcare professional has told them to do so.

More serious adverse reactions can include rash, facial swelling, trouble breathing, fainting, severe vomiting, confusion, severe weakness, severe bleeding, or an allergic reaction. Severe allergy is a medical emergency because it can progress to life-threatening swelling or airway narrowing. People should not try to treat severe allergic symptoms at home if there is breathing difficulty or facial swelling.

Medication side effects often depend on the medicine, the dose, the person’s age, the presence of other conditions, and interactions with other medicines or substances. Alcohol, some supplements, and certain foods can sometimes change how a medicine works. People with kidney or liver disease, pregnancy, or frailty may need closer monitoring. This is why healthcare professionals ask about all medicines, current symptoms, and any history of allergies.

People should be alert to warning signs that a medicine is not being tolerated. These include a rash that is spreading or painful, sudden fainting, severe chest pain, persistent vomiting, severe diarrhoea, or a major change in behaviour or mental alertness. If a person has breathlessness, throat tightness, or swelling of the lips or tongue, emergency care is needed.

It is important to report concerns promptly and not to assume that the medicine is “just part of the illness.” A side effect can be a clue that the medicine needs to be changed, the dose adjusted, or the person needs additional assessment. Written reminders and medication lists help avoid missed reporting. People should also stop and check labels when a medicine is new to them, especially if they are taking multiple medicines.

Medication safety includes knowing what to do when symptoms occur. If the reaction is mild, a healthcare professional may advise a change in dose or a switch to another medicine. If the reaction is severe, urgent medical attention is needed. Healthcare professionals can also advise what symptoms are expected and what symptoms should be taken seriously.
