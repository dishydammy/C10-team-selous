Antibiotics are medicines used to treat infections caused by bacteria. They do not work for viral infections such as most colds, coughs, or sore throats caused by viruses. Antibiotic misuse is a major public-health problem because it can lead to antibiotic resistance, meaning bacteria become less responsive to treatment. This makes future infections harder to treat and can increase complications.

Antibiotics should only be used when prescribed or recommended by a qualified healthcare professional for a bacterial infection. A clinician may choose not to prescribe antibiotics if the illness is likely viral or if there is no clear need. People should not use leftover antibiotics from a previous illness, share medicines with other people, or stop treatment early because they feel better unless a clinician has told them to do so.

People often ask when a course of antibiotics is necessary. It depends on the type of infection, severity of symptoms, age, medical history, and local guidelines. Conditions such as some ear infections, some throat infections, pneumonia, and urinary infections may need antibiotics when there is a clear bacterial cause. However, many uncomplicated viral illnesses do not need antibiotics.

Using antibiotics incorrectly can cause side effects, including nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, rash, or allergic reactions. In some cases, antibiotic use can also disrupt the normal gut bacteria and lead to secondary infections. Antibiotic resistance may also make common infections more difficult to manage in the future. For this reason, the safest approach is to use antibiotics only when they are medically indicated and to complete the course exactly as prescribed when they are prescribed.

People should also be careful about combination medicines or “broad-spectrum” options that may not be needed. A clinician will decide if the benefits outweigh the risks for the specific infection. If a person develops worsening diarrhoea, severe rash, breathing difficulty, swelling, or fever while taking antibiotics, they should seek medical advice promptly. Severe allergic reactions such as facial swelling, wheezing, or trouble breathing are emergencies.

Antibiotic stewardship is not just a hospital issue; it is also a community issue. Families, pharmacists, and clinicians all play a role in preventing unnecessary antibiotic use. Patients should ask what the medicine is for, whether it is necessary, and how long it should be taken. If there is no clear answer, they should seek clarification.

Storing antibiotics safely is important. They should be kept away from children, properly labelled, and used within their expiry date. People should never use medicines that are not prescribed to them, and medicines should never be saved for future illnesses without professional advice. Proper use supports safer treatment for everyone.
