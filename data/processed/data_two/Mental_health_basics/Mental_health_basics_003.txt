Depression is more than feeling sad for a short time. It is a common mental health condition that can affect mood, energy, sleep, appetite, concentration, and a person’s sense of value or hope. People with depression may feel persistently low, tearful, exhausted, numb, or unable to enjoy things they used to enjoy. They may also withdraw from others, lose interest in daily activities, or have changes in appetite or sleep.

Depression can be triggered by stress, loss, conflict, financial problems, chronic illness, trauma, or grief. It may also occur without an obvious trigger. It is not a sign of weakness, and it is not any person’s fault. Some people experience depression along with anxiety, while others have more symptoms of low mood and lack of energy.

Common signs include sleeping too much or too little, reduced appetite or overeating, difficulty concentrating, low self-esteem, guilt, irritability, slowed movement or speech, or thoughts that life is not worth living. In severe cases, a person may feel hopeless or think about self-harm. These symptoms deserve attention because they can become dangerous if not addressed.

A person with depression should be encouraged to seek support early. This may mean speaking with a primary care professional, counsellor, mental health worker, trusted teacher, family member, or community support service. Professional help can include talking therapy, practical support, and, when appropriate, medical review. It is important to remember that seeking help is a sign of strength, not failure.

Caregivers and family members can help by listening without judging, encouraging daily routines, offering practical support, and noticing worsening signs. If a person talks about wanting to die, harming themselves, or being unable to keep themselves safe, urgent professional help is needed. This is a crisis and should be treated seriously.
