A mental health crisis means a person is not safe, cannot cope, or is at immediate risk of harm to themselves or others. This can be triggered by severe depression, panic, trauma, substance use, psychosis, agitation, or overwhelming life stress. A crisis is not a sign of weakness; it is a sign that a person needs urgent support and safety planning.

Warning signs include talking about wanting to die, feeling unable to stay safe, hearing voices or seeing things that others do not, severe agitation, confusion, aggressive behaviour, or being unable to care for self or children. Someone may also appear detached, fearful, frantic, or unable to explain what is happening. If a person is at risk of harming themselves or someone else, urgent help is needed.

If a person is in immediate danger or a safety risk, emergency services or local crisis teams should be contacted. For children and young people, adults around them should seek urgent help if they are self-harming, threatening violence, or are unable to be calmed. In schools or community settings, staff should have a plan for urgent escalation and should never handle a potentially serious crisis alone.

Family members and caregivers can help by staying calm, staying with the person if safe to do so, removing immediate hazards, and contacting professional help. It is important to avoid arguing, threatening, or leaving the person alone if they may be unsafe. Listening without judgment and encouraging immediate professional support can reduce risk.

If there are no immediate safety concerns but the person is very distressed, a same-day assessment with a primary care professional, crisis service, or emergency mental health service may still be appropriate. Short-term support, safety planning, and follow-up are often needed after a crisis. Recovery is possible, and appropriate care can reduce risk and improve stability.
