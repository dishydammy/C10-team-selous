Stress is a normal response to pressure, change, or demands. It can be helpful in small amounts, but prolonged stress can affect sleep, mood, concentration, immune function, and relationships. Common stressors include work pressure, school demands, money problems, family conflict, caregiving responsibilities, poor sleep, or chronic illness. People can cope with stress in different ways, and what helps one person may not help another.

Healthy coping strategies include maintaining routines, taking regular breaks, eating well, sleeping, exercising in safe ways, staying connected to supportive people, and taking time to rest. Relaxation techniques such as paced breathing, mindfulness, journaling, or talking with a trusted person may provide some relief. It is also important to reduce the use of alcohol, recreational drugs, or other substances to cope with stress, as these can worsen mental and physical wellbeing.

People often ask how to know whether stress is becoming unmanageable. Signs may include persistent worry, irritability, poor concentration, excessive fatigue, panic symptoms, feeling unable to cope, or withdrawing from family and friends. If stress is affecting everyday functioning or if a person feels unsafe, they should seek support from a clinician, counsellor, teacher, or community support service.

Support from family, schools, workplaces, and communities matters. Feeling able to talk openly about stress helps prevent it from becoming overwhelming. People should not feel they must manage everything alone. Practical support, such as reducing demands, helping with tasks, or connecting the person with services, can make a difference.

This topic is broader than crisis care. The goal is to teach people how to recognize stress early and respond before it becomes severe. Mental wellbeing is strengthened by connection, rest, support, and realistic expectations.
