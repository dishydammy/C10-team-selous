Anxiety is a common emotional experience that involves worry, fear, or tension. It can be mild or severe and may be triggered by upcoming events, uncertainty, social situations, health concerns, or life stress. Panic is a more intense episode of fear that can include a racing heart, sweating, trembling, chest tightness, shortness of breath, dizziness, or a feeling of losing control. These symptoms can feel frightening and may be mistaken for a medical emergency.

Anxiety and panic are not character flaws or signs of weakness. They are common and treatable. People may also experience changes in sleep, irritability, poor concentration, or avoidance of situations that trigger fear. Over time, persistent anxiety can interfere with school, work, relationships, and daily functioning. It is important to seek support when symptoms become frequent or overwhelming.

Common triggers include work pressure, exams, financial stress, social situations, trauma reminders, or worries about health. Anxiety can sometimes include obsessive thoughts or repeated reassurance-seeking, while panic episodes may occur unexpectedly. In some people, anxiety is associated with physical symptoms such as tight muscles, stomach discomfort, or headaches.

A supportive response includes listening calmly, helping the person slow their breathing, and reminding them that the episode will usually pass even though it feels intense. Encouraging a slow, steady breathing pattern can help a person feel more grounded. It is also helpful to avoid escalating the fear by focusing on worst-case scenarios. People should seek professional help if panic episodes are frequent, if they are avoiding important activities, or if they are feeling unsafe.

If anxiety is severe, includes chest pain, persistent breathlessness, fainting, or confusion, immediate medical review is needed because some physical problems can mimic panic. Healthcare professionals can help decide whether the symptoms are primarily anxiety or if another medical issue needs evaluation. A person should also seek help if they feel unable to function, are using substances to cope, or feel like self-harm may be a risk.
