Vaccination during pregnancy can protect both the pregnant person and the baby, especially during the period when the baby is still developing in the womb and when the newborn is too young to have completed their own series. Some vaccines are recommended during pregnancy because they can help prevent severe illness in the mother and provide antibodies that may cross to the baby before birth.

Vaccines commonly discussed during pregnancy include influenza vaccination and, where recommended by local guidelines, tetanus-containing vaccines and other vaccines based on risk factors, location, and travel. Vaccination during pregnancy should be based on local recommendations and assessed on an individual basis. This is not a time to guess about the right vaccine; healthcare professionals can advise on which vaccines are appropriate and when.

Pregnant people are more vulnerable to complications from some infections, especially respiratory infections. Vaccination can reduce the risk of severe illness and can lower the chance of complications for both the pregnant person and the infant. A pregnant person who has not had a tetanus-containing vaccine or who has an uncertain vaccination record may need assessment and catch-up planning based on local guidance.

Vaccination during pregnancy is usually considered safe when recommended; however, not every vaccine is appropriate for every pregnancy. It is important to tell healthcare professionals about allergies, prior vaccine reactions, and current health problems. Vaccines may cause mild soreness, fatigue, or fever, but serious reactions are uncommon.

Pregnant people also need accurate information because misinformation can lead to delays or refusal. Vaccination is not about forcing a medical treatment; it is about preventing serious disease. For pregnant people, the decision should be informed, respectful, and based on risk, available evidence, and professional advice.

After birth, newborns may need their own immunization schedule, and the mother’s immunization status is part of this picture. Routine antenatal care often includes discussion of vaccines, infectious disease risk, and protection of the newborn. A person who is unsure about their vaccine history should talk to a clinician or local immunization service.
