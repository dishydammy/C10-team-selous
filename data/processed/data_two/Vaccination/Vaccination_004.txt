Vaccines are generally very safe. Most people have mild reactions such as soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site, low fever, tiredness, headache, or muscle ache. These reactions usually occur within a day or two and settle on their own. They are a sign that the immune system is responding and are not usually dangerous.

Serious vaccine reactions are uncommon, but they can happen. They include high fever, persistent vomiting, severe allergic reactions, fainting, or other concerning symptoms that need medical review. A person who has swelling of the face or lips, breathing difficulty, wheezing, or severe rash after vaccination should seek urgent medical help. It is important to distinguish common mild reactions from warning signs that require professional care.

Vaccines are not given lightly. Before vaccines are used in a programme, they are studied for safety and effectiveness. Monitoring continues after approval to identify rare side effects. This is why many countries have vaccine safety systems and reporting mechanisms. Even rare problems can be identified and acted on, but this does not mean the vaccine is broadly unsafe.

People may ask whether a vaccine can cause the disease it is meant to prevent. For inactivated or non-live vaccines, this is not the case. Live vaccines are carefully selected and are generally safe for most people, but they may not be appropriate for some groups, such as pregnant people or people with certain immune problems, depending on local guidance. This is why vaccine decisions are based on individual risk and professional advice.

When a person has a history of severe allergic reaction to an ingredient in a vaccine, the vaccine may need special assessment or may be avoided in some cases. People should inform healthcare providers about allergies, previous reactions, current illness, pregnancy, and any medicines they take. This helps guide safe decisions.

A child or adult with a severe illness or high fever should usually be assessed before vaccination, but mild illnesses do not necessarily prevent it. Local guidance and a clinician’s advice are important to avoid unnecessary delay while ensuring safety. Vaccination is one of the best ways to prevent serious disease, but it still needs to be administered with proper assessment and informed consent.
