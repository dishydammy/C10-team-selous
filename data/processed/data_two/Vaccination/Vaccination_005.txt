Missed or delayed vaccinations are common and do not always require restarting the whole series. In many programmes, a child or adult can catch up on missed doses without needing to repeat the earlier ones, depending on the vaccine and the timing. The key is to speak with a healthcare professional or immunization service to get a catch-up plan that is appropriate for the person’s age, vaccine history, and risk factors.

The reasons for missed vaccines vary. They may include illness, lack of access to healthcare, vaccine supply problems, travel, confusion about schedules, or misinformation. Whatever the reason, the safe response is to catch up rather than ignore the gap. Delaying protection leaves the person vulnerable to infections that can be severe.

For infants and children, vaccine catch-up can be especially important because they are vulnerable to diseases such as measles, meningitis, whooping cough, and pneumonia. Families should keep immunization records and ask for a review if they are unsure whether a child has received all recommended doses. School immunization records or local public-health clinics may also help confirm what is missing.

Adults may also need catch-up vaccination, particularly if they are travelling, work in higher-risk settings, are pregnant, or have missed routine vaccines in earlier life. Some vaccines may need booster doses over time. Clinics can assess whether a person is up to date and what should be given next.

People sometimes worry that catching up is too complicated. In many settings, simple catch-up schedules exist and are planned to be efficient. A person should not be left to decide on their own based on memory. Both professionals and public-health records can help. A brief conversation with a clinician may be enough to clarify the next step.

If a person is unsure whether a previous dose caused a problem, they should still seek professional advice rather than avoid further vaccination without assessment. Healthcare professionals can review the history and determine the safest next step. This is particularly important when a person had a previous severe reaction or an uncertain record.
