Malaria symptoms usually begin with fever and may be mistaken for a common viral illness. Many people experience chills, sweating, headache, body aches, feeling weak, nausea, or loss of appetite. The pattern can vary, but malaria often causes cycles of fever and sweating. In some people symptoms are mild at first, while in others the illness worsens rapidly.

A person with malaria may have fever, feeling cold and shivery, vomiting, diarrhoea, fatigue, or confusion in severe illness. Children may seem unusually sleepy, fussy, or difficult to wake. Pregnant people may have fever and feel weak, with a higher risk of complications. A healthcare professional should assess fever in a malaria-risk area or after travel to a risk area, especially if there are other symptoms or the person is vulnerable.

The key danger signs are those that suggest severe malaria or complications. These include repeated vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, difficulty breathing, seizures, severe weakness, confusion, severe dehydration, jaundice, or reduced consciousness. A person who is not able to drink, stays very drowsy, or is bleeding or collapsing needs urgent medical review. Severe malaria can become life-threatening quickly and requires hospital-level care.

Care-seeking should not wait for several days if symptoms are significant. A person with fever in a malaria-risk area should seek evaluation promptly, particularly if fever is high, persistent, or associated with chills, vomiting, or worsening weakness. Tests for malaria are often needed to confirm the diagnosis, and treatment should be guided by health professionals. Self-treatment without testing or without proper guidance can delay care and increase the risk of complications.

People often ask whether malaria always causes high fever. It may not. Some people have moderate fever or fluctuating symptoms. Others may feel tired or have headache and body aches without a clear diagnosis. This is why fever in malaria-risk settings should be taken seriously, especially in children and pregnant people. A person’s history of travel, mosquito exposure, recent sleep outdoors, or local outbreaks matters for assessment.

Pregnant people deserve a lower threshold for assessment because malaria can worsen quickly during pregnancy and may increase the risk of anaemia, severe illness, and poor outcomes. In areas of high transmission, prevention and early testing are especially important. People who are ill with fever during pregnancy should seek prompt assessment rather than waiting to “see if it settles.”

Children and infants need especially prompt evaluation if they have fever, poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, or breathing difficulties. Infants may not be able to communicate how unwell they feel, so caregivers should observe signs such as persistent crying, poor activity, not taking feeds, or a dry mouth. In school-age children, fever with headache, body aches, and fatigue should also be reviewed.

The general principle is that fever in a malaria-risk area should be treated as potentially important until assessed. Testing, supportive care, and appropriate treatment all depend on a proper medical assessment. Early diagnosis and treatment reduce severe complications and protect vulnerable people.
