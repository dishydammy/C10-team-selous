Infection prevention and hygiene are practical steps that lower the spread of germs in homes, schools, workplaces, and communities. They help prevent common infections such as diarrhoea, respiratory illnesses, skin infections, and eye infections. A good infection-prevention approach is to reduce exposure to germs, support immunity, and use simple habits consistently. These habits are not only useful during outbreaks; they are part of everyday health protection.

Many infections spread through droplets, contaminated hands, contaminated food or water, or contact with infected surfaces. Respiratory infections can spread when people cough, sneeze, speak, or touch shared objects. Diarrhoeal diseases often spread when hands are not clean after using the toilet or before eating. Skin infections can spread through close contact or sharing towels, razors, and personal items. Good hygiene is one of the simplest and most effective public-health measures available.

Hand hygiene is a cornerstone of infection prevention. Hands should be washed with soap and water when they are visibly dirty, after using the toilet, before preparing food, before eating, after coughing or sneezing, after handling waste, and after coming home from public places. Alcohol-based hand rub can be useful when soap and water are not available, but it does not replace handwashing when hands are visibly soiled. Children should be taught to wash hands properly using soap and clean running water, making sure to cover the whole hand, including between fingers and under nails.

Respiratory hygiene includes covering the nose and mouth when coughing or sneezing, throwing away tissues promptly, and washing hands after contact with saliva or mucus. People with fever, cough, or other symptoms should try to avoid close contact with vulnerable people when possible. Open windows or improved airflow can help reduce the buildup of respiratory droplets indoors, especially in crowded spaces. Good ventilation matters in schools, homes, and workplaces during outbreaks or high-risk periods.

Food and water safety also prevent many infections. Safe drinking water should be stored and handled properly; water that may contain contamination should be treated or boiled when advised by local health guidance. Food should be prepared with clean hands, clean surfaces, and safe temperatures. Raw foods should be kept separate from ready-to-eat foods, and leftovers should be stored and reheated safely. Cooking food thoroughly and washing fruits and vegetables when appropriate can reduce the risk of foodborne illness.

Environmental hygiene matters too. Cleaning and disinfecting frequently touched surfaces, such as door handles, toilets, taps, shared toys, and school desks, can reduce spread when there is a known outbreak or when people are ill. Households should safely manage waste, avoid sharing personal hygiene items, and keep living spaces dry and ventilated to reduce mould and other infection risks. In communities, clean public spaces and safe sanitation systems play a large role in lowering infection rates.

People may ask whether stronger cleaning products are always needed. In routine daily life, proper cleaning and regular household hygiene are usually more important than harsh or excessive disinfecting. Overuse of chemicals can irritate skin or airways and is not necessary for most settings. The priority is to clean what is used often, wash hands, and manage illness promptly. Extra cleaning is particularly useful when someone in the home is sick, has diarrhoea, or has a respiratory infection.

A person should seek healthcare advice if they have signs of severe illness, persistent fever, shortness of breath, severe diarrhoea, signs of dehydration, or worsening infection that does not improve. Caregivers should monitor vulnerable groups, especially infants, children, pregnant people, and people with chronic illnesses, as they may become more unwell faster. Early assessment can prevent complications. In schools and community settings, communication around illness, symptoms, and safe attendance helps protect others while balancing access to learning and work.

The most effective infection prevention is consistent, simple, and repeated. Good hygiene does not need to be complicated. It works best when everyone understands the basics and follows them regularly. Prevention is especially important in crowded homes, schools, health facilities, and areas with poor sanitation or limited access to safe water.
