Diarrhoeal illness is a common infectious condition that causes loose or watery stools, often with abdominal cramps, nausea, or vomiting. It is usually caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites, and it may spread through contaminated food, water, or poor hand hygiene. Diarrhoeal diseases are especially dangerous in infants, young children, and people with poor nutritional status or chronic illness because they can cause dehydration quickly.

The most important risk factors are unsafe drinking water, poor sanitation, contaminated food, poor handwashing, and close contact with an infected person. Diarrhoea may occur after travel, outbreaks in community settings, or contact with people who are sick. Foodborne and waterborne infections are common causes, especially where sanitation and safe water are limited.

The main concern is dehydration. Signs of mild dehydration may include thirst, dry mouth, slightly reduced urine output, and feeling tired. Moderate or severe dehydration may cause dizziness, confusion, reduced tears, very infrequent urination, sunken eyes, cold hands and feet, fast heartbeat, and lethargy. In children, dehydration may also be seen as irritability, poor feeding, sunken fontanelle in infants, and unusual sleepiness. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency and needs urgent care.

Care for diarrhoeal illness focuses on replacing fluids and monitoring warning signs. Oral rehydration solution is commonly used when the person can drink and is not severely unwell. In severe cases, medical care may require fluids to be given by a healthcare professional. Oral rehydration should be started early. Plain water alone may not replace the salts and sugar that are lost, so appropriate rehydration solutions are best when available. People should also continue to eat small regular meals when able, if the person is not vomiting.

A person should seek healthcare advice if diarrhoea is persistent, accompanied by fever, blood in the stool, severe abdominal pain, signs of dehydration, or not improving. Blood in the stool can signal a more serious infection and requires assessment. In infants and children, more frequent review is needed because dehydration can develop rapidly. Pregnant people and people with chronic disease should seek professional guidance earlier because they may become ill faster or have more risk from dehydration.

Good prevention includes handwashing with soap after using the toilet, before preparing food, and before eating; safe storage of drinking water; safe food preparation; breastfeeding support for infants when appropriate; and avoiding close contact with people who are vomiting or have diarrhoea. Food safety, sanitation, and clean water access are major public-health measures. Schools and communities play a key role by promoting hygiene and safe food handling.

People may ask whether anti-diarrhoeal medicines are always safe. Many such medicines are not recommended for some infections or age groups without guidance because they may slow the body’s natural clearing of germs or worsen certain conditions. When a person has fever, blood in stools, or severe illness, they should not rely on over-the-counter medicines alone. Professional assessment is important for severe or persistent cases.

The overall goal is to manage dehydration early, identify high-risk cases, and stop the spread of infection. Diarrhoeal illness is common but preventable, and early intervention makes a major difference in outcomes.
