HIV and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are spread in different ways depending on the infection, but sexual contact is a common route. Prevention, testing, and early care are central to reducing harm and preventing transmission. These infections can have serious health effects if untreated, but many can be prevented, treated, or managed effectively with appropriate care.

HIV is a virus that affects the immune system. It can be transmitted through certain body fluids, especially blood, semen, vaginal fluids, and breast milk. STIs such as gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, and herpes can also be transmitted through sexual contact or, in some cases, skin-to-skin contact. Risk increases with unprotected sex, multiple partners, sex with a person who has a treatable or untreated infection, and shared needles. Public-health prevention focuses on safer sex, testing, treatment, and access to health services.

Prevention strategies include consistent and correct condom use, mutually faithful relationships, avoiding sharing needles or other injecting equipment, and reducing the number of sexual partners when appropriate. Regular STI and HIV testing is important, especially for people with new partners, people in high-risk settings, or anyone with symptoms. Testing may be offered through primary care, community clinics, sexual health services, or public-health programmes. Care should be confidential and respectful.

Symptoms vary widely. Some people have no symptoms at all, while others may notice genital sores, discharge, pain, burning on urination, rash, fever, swollen glands, or unexplained weight loss. HIV can remain silent for years, so people may not know they are infected without testing. Early testing matters because treatment can improve long-term health and lower the chance of transmitting infection to others.

Pregnant people may need specific attention because some infections can harm both the pregnant person and the baby. HIV testing during pregnancy is often part of routine care in many settings, and treatment can prevent transmission to the baby. STIs during pregnancy may also cause complications, so early testing and care are important. Pregnant people should seek assessment if they have symptoms or have had a risk exposure.

People with possible exposure should seek testing and counselling promptly rather than waiting for symptoms to develop. This includes people who have had sex without a condom, have had a new partner, or are worried about a possible transmission event. Treatment for STIs is often available and can prevent complications. If a person has HIV, appropriate care and follow-up can help protect immune function and improve long-term health.

If a person has symptoms such as severe pelvic pain, fever, rash, sores that are painful or spreading, breathing difficulty, or confusion, urgent healthcare review is needed. Some STI symptoms can worsen quickly or indicate complications. Severe illness or a life-threatening reaction to an infection needs medical assessment without delay.

Misconceptions about HIV and STIs are common. Some people believe that symptoms always appear quickly or that a person can tell by appearance alone. In reality, many infections can be silent. Another misconception is that STIs are only a problem for certain groups; in truth, anyone who is sexually active can be affected. Accurate information, routine testing, and respectful care are essential.

The safest approach is prevention, testing, and treatment, with early care-seeking when symptoms or exposure occurs. Access to confidential screening and community support improves outcomes and reduces transmission in the wider population.
