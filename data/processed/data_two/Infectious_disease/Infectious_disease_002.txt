Malaria is a serious infection spread by mosquitoes in some regions. It can cause fever, chills, fatigue, and severe illness, and it can be life-threatening, especially in children, pregnant people, and people with limited access to healthcare. Prevention focuses on reducing mosquito bites, managing the local environment, and using appropriate protective measures when risk is present.

The main prevention methods include sleeping under an insecticide-treated bed net, using mosquito repellents when appropriate, wearing clothing that covers arms and legs in the evening, and reducing standing water where mosquitoes breed. People living or travelling in malaria-risk areas should ask local health services about the right prevention measures. In some settings, preventive medicines may be recommended for travellers or for people at high risk, but this should be guided by public-health or clinical advice. The priority is to follow locally appropriate guidance rather than use medication without professional advice.

Mosquito control helps at the household and community level. This can include clearing water collections, covering containers that hold water, managing drainage, and reducing places where mosquitoes can rest and breed. Community clean-up and environmental management, combined with personal protection, can markedly reduce mosquito exposure. Schools and community groups can also play a role by raising awareness and supporting net distribution or screening programmes where available.

Pregnant people need extra attention because malaria can be dangerous during pregnancy and can affect the mother and baby. In malaria-risk areas, routine antenatal care may include assessment of malaria risk and advice on protective measures. Pregnant people should not assume that mild illness is not serious, because fever during pregnancy may also be caused by malaria or other infections. Routine local guidance should be followed, and care should be sought early if fever or other symptoms occur.

Children are also at high risk of severe malaria. Parents and caregivers should ensure children sleep under treated nets, wear protective clothing at night if appropriate, and seek care quickly for fever or poor feeding. Infants and young children may become ill quickly, and caregivers should not wait to see if symptoms improve on their own. Symptoms such as fever, lethargy, vomiting, convulsions, or breathing difficulty need urgent assessment.

People often ask whether malaria is prevented simply by avoiding mosquitoes in the daytime. Malaria mosquitoes are usually active at dusk and night, so evening and nighttime protection is especially important. People should also check whether the home has open eaves, damaged windows, or nearby breeding sites. Bed nets should be properly hung and checked for tears, and protective measures should be used consistently.

A diagnosis of malaria should be based on medical assessment and testing when appropriate. People with fever in risk areas should not self-label it as malaria without evaluation. Other infections can cause similar symptoms. A person with severe weakness, confusion, breathing difficulty, seizures, or repeated vomiting should seek urgent medical care, especially if they live in or have recently travelled to a malaria-risk area.

Public-health strategies for malaria prevention are strongest when community knowledge, vector control, and access to treatment are all in place. Prevention is not just an individual behaviour; it also depends on local sanitation, environmental management, and health-system support. In areas with seasonal malaria, community campaigns around bed net use, early diagnosis, and rapid care-seeking can reduce illness and deaths.
