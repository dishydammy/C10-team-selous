Asthma is a chronic lung condition that causes the airways to become inflamed and sensitive. When triggered, the airways can narrow, making breathing difficult. It is common in children and adults, and it can range from mild to severe. People with asthma may have episodes of wheezing, chest tightness, cough, or shortness of breath. The condition is usually long-term and requires ongoing attention, but many people can control it well with proper care and avoidance of triggers.

Asthma symptoms can be triggered by viral infections, pollen, dust, smoke, air pollution, cold air, exercise, strong smells, mould, pets, or allergens. Stress can also worsen symptoms in some people. People with asthma may experience symptoms at night, with exercise, or during colds. Some people have mild intermittent symptoms; others have frequent symptoms that need daily therapy and monitoring. The main goal is to keep airways open and prevent severe attacks.

Recognising asthma often starts with a history of recurrent cough, wheeze, chest tightness, or shortness of breath, especially when triggered by exercise or viral illnesses. A clinician may ask about family history, allergies, smoking exposure, housing conditions, and patterns of symptoms. Diagnosis often involves reviewing symptoms and sometimes breathing tests. It is important not to diagnose asthma without a proper medical assessment, because other conditions can cause similar symptoms.

Asthma management usually includes learning personal triggers, avoiding exposures when possible, and using prescribed preventive or reliever treatment according to professional instructions. Regular follow-up with a healthcare professional helps decide whether the plan is working. People should also understand when symptoms are worsening and when urgent care is needed. An asthma action plan is often useful, especially for school-age children and adults with recurring symptoms.

Prevention focuses on reducing exposure to smoke, controlling dampness and mould in homes, avoiding known allergens where possible, and treating other conditions that can worsen breathing such as rhinitis or viral infections. Vaccination against influenza and other relevant infections may also reduce triggers for some people. People with asthma should be encouraged to keep up with routine healthcare, and if symptoms are not well controlled, they should discuss this with a professional.

Warning signs of worsening asthma include symptoms that occur more often, need reliever medicine more often, or wake the person up at night. Breathing may become more laboured, the person may speak in short phrases, or the chest may feel tight and the lips may look blue or grey in severe episodes. In severe asthma, a person may not be able to speak full sentences, may have severe distress, or may appear confused. This needs urgent medical assessment.

Child, school, and community settings are important for asthma management. In schools, staff should know which children have asthma and how to respond if symptoms worsen. Families also benefit from education on trigger control, medication storage, and emergency plans. Good asthma care combines prevention, symptom monitoring, professional review, and clear action plans.
