Hypertension, or high blood pressure, is a common chronic condition that raises the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, and other complications. It often develops gradually and may not cause obvious symptoms, which is why it is often called a “silent” condition. People may feel fine for years while blood pressure remains elevated, so regular measurement matters.

Blood pressure is measured in two numbers: systolic pressure when the heart beats and diastolic pressure between beats. A healthcare professional can explain what a person’s numbers mean and whether action is needed. Elevated blood pressure can be caused by lifestyle factors, stress, excess salt intake, obesity, alcohol, smoking, family history, kidney disease, or other health conditions. Some people also develop hypertension during pregnancy, which needs specialist assessment and careful monitoring.

Symptoms are not always present, but some people may notice headaches, dizziness, nosebleeds, or a feeling of pressure. These signs are not always specific and should not be relied on to decide whether blood pressure is high. Routine checks are the main way to identify hypertension. Screening is particularly important for adults, especially those with obesity, diabetes, kidney disease, or a family history of cardiovascular disease.

Prevention and management focus on regular measurement, healthy diet, reducing salt, physical activity, weight management, limiting alcohol, avoiding tobacco, and treating other risk factors. Good sleep and stress reduction also matter. Some people need prescription medication, but the best approach is tailored to the individual by a clinician. Medication should only be started or adjusted by a qualified healthcare professional.

Monitoring at home may be useful when a clinician advises it. People should learn how to measure blood pressure correctly, rest before measuring, and keep a record of readings. Home monitoring helps identify patterns and informs follow-up. New or severe symptoms such as severe headache, confusion, chest pain, shortness of breath, or sudden weakness require immediate medical attention.

Blood pressure can also worsen in some special situations. For example, blood pressure during pregnancy needs particular attention. A person with severe headache, visual changes, swelling, or seizures should seek urgent care because high blood pressure complications can become life-threatening. Meanwhile, in adults with chronic hypertension, signs of poor control include persistent headaches, fatigue, or increasing complications such as eye changes or kidney problems.

Primary care teams often help with checking blood pressure, planning lifestyle changes, and deciding if medication is needed. Community screening, pharmacy checks, and public health programmes can also help identify people with untreated hypertension. The key point is that high blood pressure is common, often manageable, and most dangerous when it is unrecognised or untreated.
