Some pregnancy problems are common, but a few can become serious if not recognised early. Pregnancy complications include conditions that may affect the pregnant person, the baby, or both. Well-known examples include pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, miscarriage, placenta problems, and severe infection. Other concerns may include blood clots, anaemia, severe nausea and vomiting, high blood pressure, and abnormal fetal growth. Screening, early assessment, and follow-up are essential because symptoms can progress quickly.

Pre-eclampsia is a serious condition that often causes high blood pressure and may affect organs such as the kidneys, liver, and brain. It can occur after 20 weeks of pregnancy and sometimes after birth. Warning signs include severe headache, visual changes, swelling, upper abdominal pain, sudden weight gain, or shortness of breath. Risk factors include first pregnancy, multiple pregnancy, previous pre-eclampsia, age extremes, obesity, and certain chronic conditions. This condition requires medical evaluation and may need urgent hospital management.

Gestational diabetes is elevated blood sugar that develops during pregnancy. Some pregnant people have no symptoms, which is why screening is important. Risk factors include obesity, family history of diabetes, previous large baby, age, and certain ethnic backgrounds depending on regional patterns. Symptoms can include excessive thirst, frequent urination, blurred vision, or recurrent infections, but many people experience no obvious symptoms. Early identification helps reduce risks to the mother and baby and supports monitoring of blood sugar, nutrition, and fetal growth.

Other important complications include bleeding in pregnancy, placenta problems, preterm labour, severe hypertension, severe infection, and reduced fetal movement. Symptoms may include vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, back pain, leaking fluid, painful contractions, fever, or a sudden reduction in baby movement. Some people may develop symptoms of severe illness very quickly. A healthcare professional should assess any sudden or unusual change in how the person feels.

Monitoring for complications includes regular antenatal visits, blood pressure checks, urine tests where needed, blood sugar screening, and assessment of symptoms. People with chronic diseases such as high blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease, or autoimmune conditions may need closer follow-up. Family and caregivers can support attendance at visits, medication adherence when prescribed, and early reporting of symptoms.

Care-seeking is essential if warning signs develop. Severe symptoms such as persistent severe headache, fits, chest pain, severe shortness of breath, heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, or sudden swelling should lead to urgent assessment. If a person feels unwell or has a marked change in fetal movement, health professionals should be contacted promptly. Timely management reduces risk and helps ensure that serious complications are identified before they become life-threatening.

Pregnancy complications are not always obvious, which is why antenatal care matters. Early recognition, blood pressure monitoring, blood tests, screening, and clear instructions for warning signs can make a major difference. Good communication with healthcare professionals is one of the strongest protections against severe outcomes.
