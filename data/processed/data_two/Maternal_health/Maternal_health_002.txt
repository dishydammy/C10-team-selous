Pregnancy causes many changes in the body. Some symptoms are common and expected, while others may indicate a problem that requires medical assessment. Many pregnant people experience fatigue, breast tenderness, nausea or vomiting, mood changes, constipation, increased urination, food aversions, heartburn, pelvic pressure, and mild dizziness. These may be uncomfortable but not necessarily dangerous. However, they should be discussed if they are severe, sudden, ongoing, or accompanied by other red flags.

Early pregnancy often includes fatigue, nausea, and vomiting. Mild nausea is common and may improve with smaller meals, regular fluids, avoiding greasy foods, and eating when appetite allows. Severe vomiting or inability to keep liquids down may lead to dehydration and requires prompt assessment. Breast tenderness, mood swings, and sleep changes are also common but may be distressing. Rest, reassurance, and support from family and healthcare professionals can help.

In the second and third trimesters, symptoms may include backache, leg cramps, swelling of the feet or ankles, heartburn, shortness of breath with activity, and increased pelvic pressure. Mild swelling can be common, especially later in the day, but sudden or severe swelling can be concerning. Many people also feel movements from the baby later in pregnancy, and a change in movement patterns should be checked if significant.

Safe general self-care during pregnancy includes rest, hydration, regular meals, gentle exercise if approved by a healthcare professional, good hygiene, and a balanced diet. Daily activities should be adjusted if there is dizziness, pelvic pain, or unusual fatigue. Avoiding smoking, vaping, alcohol, and recreational drugs is important. Pregnant people should also avoid unnecessary medicines or supplements; any medication, including vitamins or herbal products, should be discussed with a clinician or pharmacist.

Many self-care questions are common. For example, people ask whether mild constipation is expected, whether headaches are normal, and whether mild spotting is okay. Some symptoms can be common but still need review. Any vaginal bleeding, persistent severe abdominal pain, fever, persistent vomiting, severe or worsening headache, visual changes, fainting, or chest pain needs medical advice. Some symptoms are not typical of pregnancy alone and can signal infection, anaemia, blood pressure problems, or miscarriage risk.

It is also useful to make a distinction between ordinary pregnancy changes and symptoms that may reflect serious illness. Mild nausea may be expected, but severe vomiting with inability to drink or signs of dehydration is not. Mild tiredness may be common, but worsening weakness, breathlessness, or confusion is not. Mild lower back discomfort can be common, but constant severe pain or pain with bleeding is not. Occasional swelling may be expected, but sudden swelling of the face, hands, or feet, especially with headache or visual symptoms, should be assessed promptly.

Pregnant people should know when to discuss symptoms with a healthcare professional even if the symptoms are not emergency-level. This includes persistent fever, severe tiredness, worsening dizziness, shortness of breath, significant weight gain or swelling, heavy discharge, severe itching, or increasing pain. Asking for advice early is safer than waiting until symptoms become severe. When in doubt, it is reasonable to seek assessment, especially if symptoms are unusual, escalating, or affecting ability to eat, drink, or rest.

Supportive care at home includes monitoring symptoms, resting, keeping a schedule of appointments, and arranging follow-up. Family and caregivers can help by noticing warning signs, assisting with transportation, checking whether the pregnant person is eating and drinking well, and encouraging timely contact with healthcare services. Pregnancy self-care should be proactive, especially when symptoms change quickly or become intense.
