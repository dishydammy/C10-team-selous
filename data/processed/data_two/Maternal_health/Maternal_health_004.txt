Anaemia during pregnancy is a common problem caused by lower than normal levels of haemoglobin, which reduces the blood’s ability to carry oxygen. Pregnancy increases the body’s need for iron and other nutrients, and some people develop anaemia because of iron deficiency, poor dietary intake, blood loss, repeated pregnancies, or other medical conditions. Anaemia can affect energy, concentration, heart function, and the body’s ability to cope with pregnancy and birth.

Common symptoms of anaemia include tiredness, weakness, pale skin, dizziness, feeling cold, shortness of breath with exertion, palpitations, poor concentration, headaches, or feeling unusually faint. Some people may not have symptoms early on. Anaemia is often identified through routine blood tests during antenatal care. Certain groups are at increased risk, including people with poor dietary intake, heavy periods before pregnancy, multiple pregnancies close together, vomiting or severe nausea, bleeding during pregnancy, or chronic illnesses that affect iron absorption or blood formation.

Screening is an important part of ordinary antenatal care. If anaemia is suspected, a clinician may order a blood count and assess iron levels, bleeding, diet, and other contributing factors. In many settings, testing is repeated later in pregnancy, especially if there are symptoms or risk factors. Prevention focuses on appropriate nutrition, treatment of underlying blood loss or illness, and follow-up to confirm improvement. Iron supplementation is sometimes prescribed when clinically indicated, but the exact choice and dose should be guided by a clinician.

Nutrition during pregnancy should support maternal health and fetal growth. A balanced diet includes enough energy, protein, iron, folate, iodine, calcium, and other nutrients. Foods rich in iron include legumes, green leafy vegetables, lean meats, fish when appropriate, and iron-fortified foods. Vitamin C-rich foods can help iron absorption when eaten with meals. Folate is important before and during pregnancy; foods such as leafy greens, beans, and fortified grains may help, and supplementation may be recommended by a clinician when appropriate. Adequate iodine is also important for thyroid function and brain development.

Common questions include whether pregnant people need extra calories, whether plant-based diets are safe, and what to do about food aversions. In general, most people need additional nutrients rather than simply more calories, and diet quality matters. If the person has restrictive dietary patterns, chronic illness, or severe nausea, assessment is important. Some conditions such as pica, severe appetite loss, vegetarian or vegan dietary patterns, and gastrointestinal disease may need tailored advice.

General management principles for anaemia in pregnancy include adequate rest, eating regular nutritious meals, treating the cause of the anaemia, and following up with healthcare professionals. If a pregnant person has symptoms such as dizziness, fainting, chest pain, shortness of breath at rest, or heavy bleeding, urgent medical review is needed. Severe anaemia can lead to complications and should not be treated casually.

Good nutrition is also important for preventing low birth weight and supporting maternal energy and recovery. Pregnant people should be encouraged to drink water regularly, eat protein, fruits, vegetables, and iron-rich foods, and maintain a routine with prenatal vitamins or supplements when advised by a clinician. The safest approach is individualized professional guidance, especially when there are nutritional deficiencies, symptoms, multiple pregnancies, or chronic health conditions.
