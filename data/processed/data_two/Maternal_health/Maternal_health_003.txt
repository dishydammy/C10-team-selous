Pregnancy can be accompanied by symptoms that are expected, but some symptoms mean the mother or the pregnancy may be at risk. These are sometimes called danger signs. They should never be ignored because early assessment can prevent serious complications. Danger signs include heavy bleeding, severe abdominal or pelvic pain, severe headache, visual changes, fits or seizures, significant swelling, severe shortness of breath, severe weakness, fever, or reduced fetal movement when relevant.

Heavy bleeding or bleeding that soaks pads, is ongoing, or is associated with pain or fainting should prompt urgent assessment. Any bleeding in pregnancy should be discussed with a clinician, but bleeding that is heavy or accompanied by severe pain, dizziness, or collapse needs immediate attention. Similarly, severe abdominal or pelvic pain may be concerning and may signal ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, placental problems, severe infection, or other serious issues. Pain that is sudden, intense, persistent, or associated with bleeding should not be managed as if it were routine discomfort.

Severe headache, blurred vision, flashing lights, or sudden visual changes are important warning signs, especially if they occur with swelling or high blood pressure concerns. Fits or seizures are emergencies. They require urgent medical attention, and the person may need emergency transport. Severe swelling of the face, hands, or feet, especially if it is sudden or accompanied by headache, visual disturbance, abdominal pain, or difficulty breathing, should be assessed urgently.

Reduced fetal movement later in pregnancy can be concerning when the change is clear and persistent. If movement becomes much less than usual, or stops, it is important to seek professional assessment. The exact threshold is not always the same for every person or pregnancy; the key issue is a marked reduction or sudden change that is unusual for that person. Healthcare professionals can guide this assessment.

Severe illness can also present as fever, confusion, severe weakness, chest pain, or shortness of breath. Chest pain or significant breathing difficulty during pregnancy needs emergency assessment. Severe nausea and vomiting leading to dehydration, inability to keep fluids down, or signs of poor hydration are also urgent. Pregnancy-related danger signs may develop quickly, so prompt action matters.

When at home, a pregnant person or caregiver should know what to do if one of these symptoms occurs. If there is severe bleeding, severe pain, fits, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or signs of shock, emergency help should be sought immediately. This may mean going to a hospital or calling emergency services if available. If symptoms are concerning but not life-threatening, a same-day assessment with a local clinician or maternity service may be appropriate. It is important not to delay care while trying to self-treat.

Many people worry about whether mild discomfort is significant. Mild cramps, stretching sensations, or occasional aches can be part of pregnancy. However, new or worsening pain that is severe, constant, or associated with bleeding, fever, fainting, or reduced movement needs review. A healthcare professional can help decide whether symptoms are routine or urgent. A plan for emergency transport and a list of emergency contacts should be prepared in advance for all pregnancies, especially if there are risk factors.
